## ABSTRACT 

Accurate analysis of electron microscopy (EM) images is essential for exploring nanoscale biological structures, yet data heterogeneity and fragmented workflows hinder scalable insights. Pretrained on large, diverse datasets, image foundation models provide a robust framework for learning transferable representations across tasks. Here, we introduce EM-DINO, the first EM image foundational model pretrained on EM-5M, a large curated and standardized EM corpus (5 million images) encompassing multiple species, tissues, protocols, and resolutions. EM-DINO’s multi-scale embeddings capture rich image features that support multiple applications, including organ-specific pattern recognition, image deduplication, and high quality image restoration. Building on these representations, we developed OmniEM, a U-shaped architecture for unified dense prediction that achieves superior performance compared with task-specific models in both image restoration and segmentation. In restoration benchmarks, OmniEM matches the performance of the EM-specific diffusion model while reducing spurious structural artifacts that could mislead interpretation. It also outperforms previous methods across 2D and 3D mitochondrial segmentation, as well as multi-class organelle segmentation tasks. Furthermore, we demonstrate OmniEM’s integrated capability to generate high-resolution segmentations from low-resolution inputs, offering the potential to enable fine-scale subcellular analysis in legacy and high-throughput EM datasets. Together, EM-5M, EM-DINO, OmniEM, and an integrated Napari plugin comprise a comprehensive end-to-end toolkit for standardized EM analysis, advancing cellular and subcellular understanding and accelerating the discovery of novel organelle morphologies and disease-related alterations.

## Main 16 

Electron microscopy (EM) provides an unparalleled window into the nanometer-scale architecture of biological systems, 17 

revealing subcellular organelles, synaptic connections, and tissue ultrastructure with extraordinary detail 1–6 . In recent years, 18 

high-throughput EM has transformed our ability to visualize cellular and tissue organization at this resolution. While traditional 19 

2D EM relies on expert interpretation of densely packed organelles, volumetric EM (vEM) techniques have emerged over 20 

the past two decades to satisfy connectomics’ demand for large-scale neuronal reconstructions—generating datasets far too 21 

extensive for manual analysis or hypothesis testing 7–9 . Consequently, automated methods are indispensable in EM workflows, 22 

spanning low-level tasks (restoration, alignment), segmentation, and higher-order, question-driven analyses such as mapping 23 

synaptic connectivity at the circuit level. Unlike fluorescence microscopy, where semantic content derives from labels or 24 

contrast agents, EM images are grayscale and rich with nanometer-scale structural information. Therefore, EM research 25 

demands bespoke computational models and automated tools to fully harness modern EM’s potential and address the pressing 26 

biological questions it can illuminate 10 .27 

Modern deep-learning (DL) methods have driven substantial progress in EM image analysis, automating tasks such as 28 

segmentation, denoising, and registration 11–16 . Task-specific models now achieve expert-level performance on benchmarks 29 

For example, neuronal reconstruction (SNEMI3D 17 , CREMI 18 ), organelle identification (MitoNet 13 , BetaSeg 19 ), and image 30 

restoration (EMDiffuse 15 ) . However, these approaches depend on narrowly defined training sets and often degrade when 31 

applied to new imaging conditions or biological contexts 10 . Moreover, EM workflows inherently require distinct models at 32 

each processing stage (restoration, alignment, segmentation, and higher-level analysis) imposing significant overhead for 33 

tool integration and adaptation 1, 20, 21 , and confining DL solutions to limited task domains (e.g., specialized connectomics 34 

pipelines 22 ). As a result, existing DL strategies lack the general-purpose flexibility needed for scalable, reproducible EM 35 

analysis. 36 

Self-supervised image foundation models (FMs) offer a promising path toward unified, scalable EM workflows. By 37

To address these gaps, we introduce a modular EM foundation pipeline comprising: 47 

• EM-5M : A deduplicated, stratified corpus of 5 million 2D EM images drawn from 500 TB of raw data (EMPAIR 30 ,48 

• EM-DINO : A vision transformer pretrained on EM-5M via the DINOv2 self-distillation framework 31 , producing robust, 49 

multi-scale embeddings that capture ultrastructural features. 50 

• OmniEM : A U-shaped hybrid network that consumes EM-DINO’s final and intermediate patch embeddings and 51 

supports universal dense prediction EM tasks. OmniEM achieves strong and competitive performance in denoising, 52 

super-resolution, single-organelle, and multi-organelle segmentation, with native support for both 2D slice-based inputs 53 

and 3D volumetric EM data. 54

Figure 1 offers a schematic overview of our workflow: large-scale data curation, self-supervised pretraining, and downstream 58 

task adaptation. Although foundation-model strategies are well established in natural and medical imaging, EM data pose 59 

unique challenges that vEM produces many nearly identical, contiguous slices (< 70 nm apart), so naively including adjacent 60 

patches would introduce data redundancy and imbalance, degrading dataset quality 32 . To mitigate this, we devised a bespoke 61 

deduplication pipeline that clusters patches by EM-DINO embeddings or spatial proximity, followed by uninformative filtering 62 

(Extended Data Table 1). The data cleaning and model training loop, alternating between dataset refinement and EM-DINO 63 

checkpoint updates, yields a highly diverse, nonredundant EM-5M corpus and a robust foundational model(Fig. 1, Left). 64 

Next, we use self-supervised learning to pretrain EM-DINO on EM-5M (Figure 1, Middle Up). In organ classification 65 

benchmarks, EM-DINO embeddings formed distinct clusters reflecting organ-specific morphology (Figure 1, Middle Bottom), 66 

confirming that large, heterogeneous EM data produce transferable, multi-scale representations. In principle, the EM-DINO 67 

embedding space can serve as a unified representation supporting low-level processing (alignment, denoising, super-resolution), 68 

mid-level understanding (cell and organelle segmentation), and high-level analysis (connectomics, spatial organization of 69 

subcellular structures). 70 

To operationalize this potential, we developed OmniEM, which translates EM-DINO embeddings into dense predictions 71 

across multiple tasks (Figure 1, Right Up). OmniEM’s architecture extracts patch-level features from EM-DINO’s final and 72 

intermediate layers, upsamples them via a multi-scale decoder, and applies task-specific heads for restoration or segmentation. 73 

We demonstrate OmniEM’s utility in denoising, super-resolution, mitochondrial segmentation, and multi-class segmentation 74 

(Figure 1, Right Middle) . Finally, we integrated pretrained OmniEM checkpoints into Napari-OmniEM for seamless deployment 75 

(Figure 1, Right Bottom). 76 

Overall, this work proposes a pipeline for EM image representation learning that combines large-scale self-supervised 77 

learning with EM-specific data curation. We publicly release the full toolkit, including EM-5M, EM-DINO, OmniEM, as well 78 

as data curation scripts, along with an online service for deploying pretrained segmentation models. These resources support a 79 

wide range of community use, from out-of-the-box applications to developing new downstream tasks and expanding curated 80 

EM datasets. In the sections that follow, we first evaluate EM-DINO embeddings via organ classification benchmarks (Figure 81 

2). We then assess OmniEM’s performance on key downstream tasks—image restoration (Figure 3) and both mitochondrial 82 

and multi-organelle segmentation (Figure 4). Lastly, we present practical applications, illustrating how OmniEM’s modules can 83 

be combined in compound workflows and deployed via the Napari plugin (Figure 5). Collectively, these results highlight the 84 

power of EM foundation models to enable scalable, reproducible, end-to-end automation and user-guided refinement in EM 85 

analysis. Given the diversity of EM imaging modalities, we anticipate this study will provide a strong reference foundation for 86 

more general-purpose approaches to EM image understanding, with further improvements expected as larger datasets become 87 

available. 88

## Results 89 

Evaluating Image Embeddings 90 

To quantitatively assess the impact of EM-specific pretraining, we designed two complementary experiments. First, we used 91 

organ classification to test whether the learned embeddings can distinguish EM images from different organ types. Second, we 92 

examined how embeddings capture structure within a single EM image by dividing large-field images into smaller regions 93 

and visualizing their relationships. These experiments assess both differences across datasets and internal organization within 94 

individual EM images, supporting the ability of the pretrained embeddings to capture meaningful structural information about 95 

biological tissues and cells at the EM image level. 96 

First, we evaluated image representations using two complementary organ classification tasks to assess how EM-specific 97 

pretraining influences embedding quality under different contextual conditions. The first, a context-aware task, used images 98 

from 11 organ types within EM - 5M that retained rich morphological cues (Fig.2A). The second, a fine-grained task, employed 99 

small field-of-view images from Histology Guide 1, which offer limited contextual information and present a more challenging 100 

generalization scenario for models (Fig.2D). In the context-aware task, all models exceeded 85% accuracy with just 10% of 101 

training data and surpassed 95% with full supervision, with all EM - DINO variants outperforming the DINOv2 baseline (Fig.2B). 102 

Visualization of frozen embeddings further confirmed EM-DINO’ s advantage, revealing well-separated, semantically coherent 103 

clusters compared to the more disorganized DINOv2 embeddings (Fig.2C). In the more difficult fine-grained task, performance 104 

dropped across the board due to reduced context, yet EM -DINO maintained over 70% accuracy even with minimal supervision, 105 

outperforming all other models including DINOv2, which fell below 50%(Fig.2E). With increased training data, EM - DINO 106 

rapidly surpassed 90% accuracy and converged earlier than its counterparts. Consistent with this trend, two-dimensional 107 

UMAP visualizations of the embedding space showed clearer and more compact clusters for EM-DINO (Fig.2F), while t-SNE 108 

projections yielded similar results with improved class separation compared to other models (Extended Fig.3). . Together, these 109 

results demonstrate that EM-specific self-supervised pretraining yields robust image embeddings that remain robust across a 110 

wide range of morphological and contextual variations in EM data . 111 

Next, to assess whether image embeddings capture internal spatial organization within single EM images, we partitioned 112 

large-field EM images into overlapping patches, extracted embeddings, and projected them onto three principal components 113 

using PCA for RGB visualization (Extended Fig. 4A). In this representation, patches with similar embeddings appear 114 

with similar colors, enabling direct comparison between embedding organization and underlying ultrastructure shown in 115 

the corresponding large-field images and regions of interest. We applied this analysis to representative EM images from 116 

Histology Guide, including cardiac muscle 2 and bone marrow megakaryocytes 3. In cardiac muscle (Extended Fig. 4, B 117 

left), EM-DINO embeddings separated muscle from surrounding tissue and further resolved distinct myofibril sectioning 118 

patterns, corresponding to fibers cut in longitudinal versus cross-sectional orientations, consistent with known ultrastructural 119 

organization of sarcomeres and myofibrils in cardiomyocytes(Extended Fig. 4, C left), 33 . By contrast, DINOv2 embeddings 120 

failed to consistently distinguish muscle regions or capture these orientation-dependent differences (Extended Fig. 4D left). 121 

Similarly, EM-DINO delineated nuclear lobes in bone marrow megakaryocyte 34 , whereas DINOv2 produced fragmented and 122 

noisy representations(Extended Fig. 4, B D right). Together, these results demonstrate that EM-DINO embeddings preserve 123 

biologically meaningful spatial and contextual organization within large-field EM images across diverse tissue types. 124 

Together, these experiments demonstrate that EM-DINO learns robust and transferable image representations, capturing both 125 

global differences across diverse EM datasets and fine-grained spatial organization within individual images. This capability 126 

positions EM-DINO as a general-purpose representation backbone for a wide range of downstream EM analyses. 127 

Robust Image Restoration with OmniEM 128 

High-quality image restoration is fundamental to EM analysis, because high-throughput EM techniques often sacrifice resolution 129 

or dwell time (DT) for throughput, precluding reliable detection of fine ultrastructural features. Traditional restoration methods 130 

rely on regression, but regression-based models tend to oversmooth images and erase critical nanoscale details. More recently, 131 

a generative approach, EMDiffuse 15 , has shown promise; however, its diffusion-based process may introduce hallucinated 132 

artifacts that mislead downstream analyses. To overcome these challenges, we implemented both denoising and super-resolution 133 

within the OmniEM framework, incorporating embeddings extracted from a pretrained EM-DINO model. We evaluated its 134 

performance by comparing it with EMDiffuse 15 and PSSR 35, 36 , representing diffusion-based and regression-based approaches, 135 

respectively. From the results, OmniEM achieves comparable or superior performance to PSSR and EMDiffuse in denoising 136 

and super-resolution tasks (Fig. 3). 137 

Specifically, we introduce a loss function based on embeddings derived from EM-DINO representations to encourage 138 

the preservation of fine structural details while mitigating over-smoothing. This design enables OmniEM, a primarily 139

In the denoising task, the three models exhibit distinct restoration behaviors (Fig. 3), with differences becoming particularly 151 

pronounced under high-noise conditions (Fig. 3C): PSSR preserves general morphology but misses structural details; EMDiffuse 152 

recovers visually plausible textures but include spurious artifacts not found in the long-DT ground truth; in contrast, OmniEM 153 

conservatively restores key cellular topologies with minimal spurious structural artifacts, preserving structural consistency 154 

essential for downstream tasks. The artifacts during restoration can compromise the validity of downstream analyses. For 155 

example in a vesicle segmentation task(Extended Data Fig. 6 A), a nnU-Net tends to detect more vesicles from EMDiffuse 156 

images, but with a high false-positive rate. In contrast, although OmniEM yields fewer detections (reflecting a more conservative 157 

restoration), the majority of its detected instances are accurate, resulting in substantially higher precision. These observations 158 

are corroborated by precision–recall and ROC analyses (Extended Data Fig. 6 B and C), in which OmniEM achieves larger 159 

areas under the curves, indicating more accurate and reliable segmentation. 160 

To quantitatively assess restoration quality, we evaluated perceptual similarity (LPIPS), structural similarity (Feature 161 

Similarity Index Measure, FSIM), and image resolution both using ground-truth–independent (decorrelation-based) metrics and 162 

ground-truth–dependent (Fourier ring correlation, FRC), consistent with metrics used in EMDiffuse. (Fig. 3B and D). Under all 163 

noise levels, OmniEM achieved the lowest LPIPS values among the three models (OmniEM < EMDiffuse < PSSR), indicating 164 

the highest perceptual fidelity to the ground truth (Fig. 3B and D and Extended Data Fig. 7). For FSIM, OmniEM exhibited 165 

best scores under high- and medium-noise conditions (EMDiffuse < PSSR < OmniEM)(Fig. 3 D and Extended Data Fig. 7), 166 

reflecting its ability to preserve structural details without excessive smoothing. However, under low-noise conditions(Fig. 3 B), 167 

PSSR achieved better results,possibly because its regression-based formulation is sufficient when the input images already 168 

contain rich fine structural details. Decorrelation-based resolution (Fig. 3B and D and Extended Data Fig. 7), which assesses 169 

structural sharpness independently of ground truth, showed intermediate values for OmniEM (EMDiffuse < OmniEM < PSSR), 170 

consistent with the diffusion process facilitating the generation of high-frequency structural information. However, FRC-based 171 

analysis showed that OmniEM surpassed EMDiffuse (Fig. 3B and D and Extended Data Fig. 7), suggesting that OmniEM could 172 

recover more ground-truth-related details. Unexpectedly, PSSR achieved the highest FRC-based resolution, likely because 173 

the regression-based design smooths the entire image, making it less affected by noise. A similar phenomenon was observed 174 

when α and β were set to 0, yielding higher FRC-based resolution than that obtained with α and β were set to 0.25 (data not 175 

shown). Combining the two resolution metrics, OmniEM exhibited intermediate numerical resolution values, while qualitatively 176 

achieving a balance between the over-generation of EMDiffuse and the over-smoothing of PSSR. Paired comparisons across 177 

image tiles (lines between scatters) and statistical analyses confirmed that OmniEM provided a robust compromise between 178 

perceptual accuracy and structural preservation. 179 

Beyond denoising, OmniEM exhibits consistent performance in super-resolution (Fig. 3E–H), comparable to its behavior 180 

in the denoising task. OmniEM reconstructs critical subcellular details from low-resolution inputs under high-noise (Fig. 181 

3E), low-noise (Fig. 3G), and medium-noise (Extended Data Fig. 7C). By contrast, PSSR yields overly smoothed results 182 

while EMDiffuse recovers fine details but occasionally introduces artificial textures absent from the high-resolution reference 183 

. For example, in Fig. 3G, EMDiffuse produces a membrane-like structure extending from the central mitochondrion that, 184 

while visually consistent with biological texture, lacks physiological meaning and is absent in the reference image. By 185 

comparison, OmniEM enhances ultrastructural features relative to PSSR while avoiding the spurious artifacts observed in 186 

EMDiffuse. Quantitative evaluation across image tiles consistently supports the trends observed in the qualitative analysis. (Fig. 187 

3F and H). OmniEM consistently outperforms EMDiffuse in LPIPS, FSIM and FRC-based spatial resolution metrics, which 188 

emphasize perceptual, feature-related, and structural accuracy relative to reference images. It also significantly surpasses PSSR 189 

in decorrelation-based resolution, which reflects the intrinsic spatial resolution of the images themselves. Paired comparisons 190 

and statistical analyses further demonstrate that OmniEM consistently balances detail enhancement with faithful reconstruction, 191 

yielding super-resolved images that are both structurally accurate and perceptually closer to the high-resolution reference. 192 

In conclusion, OmniEM delivers image restoration performance comparable to previous well-performing methods while 193 

avoiding model over-interpretation that could mislead downstream analyses, especially under high-noise. By producing more 194

Precise image segmentation 198 

As expert annotations are costly and limited to specific datasets, accurate organelle segmentation forms the basis for quantitative 199 

ultrastructural analysis and biological discovery, while existing methods struggle to generalize across diverse EM datasets 200 

and semantic categories. To explicitly evaluate this capability, we employed mitochondrial segmentation tasks and a multi-201 

class segmentation task , with training and testing data drawn from different EM volumes to test generalization to unseen 202 

data . Further, we benchmark OmniEM on 3D EM segmentation to evaluate how 2D-pretrained EM-DINO embeddings 203 

transfer to volumetric settings. Since EM-DINO is trained only on 2D images, a naïve baseline applies slice-wise inference 204 

by cropping volumes along the z-axis and mapping 2D predictions back into 3D space. Beyond this baseline, OmniEM 205 

supports volumetric training by integrating limited z-axis context within its intermediate feature blocks while still relying on 206 

2D-pretrained embeddings. Accordingly, we report two settings: 2D experiments, with 2D training/inference and 3D volume 207 

evaluation, and 3D experiments, with both training and inference performed on 3D volumes. Again, these findings highlight the 208 

model’s potential as a general-purpose segmentation framework for EM images. 209 

Mitochondrial morphology and distribution serve as critical indicators of cellular metabolic state and disease progression. 210 

However, the substantial morphological heterogeneity of mitochondria across tissues poses a major challenge for cross-dataset 211 

generalization in automated segmentation models 37 . In this work, we evaluated OmniEM on two complementary benchmarks. 212 

The first is the CEM-MitoLab dataset, which comprises labeled 2D EM images from multiple sources and is used to assess 2D 213 

training with evaluation on seven external 2D and 3D datasets withheld from training 13 . The second benchmark is MitoEM 38 ,214 

which provides two annotated EM volumes from different species (rat and human). We trained models on rat sub-volumes and 215 

evaluated on the human volume, forming a cross-species challenging 3D training and 3D evaluation setting. For comparison, 216 

we include MitoNet 13 as a 2D-only method and MedNeXt 39 as a 3D-only model, while nnU-Net 40 and OmniEM are evaluated 217 

under both 2D and 3D training/inference conditions. 218 

In results, the publicly released MitoNet checkpoint maintains strong segmentation accuracy across all seven benchmarks on 219 

the CEM-MitoLab, as expected(Figure 4A, third row; light-apricot histogram bars in Figure 4B). OmniEM, however, achieves 220 

comparable or superior performance to MitoNet on five of seven datasets (Figure 4A, below row; light-blue histogram bars in 221 

Figure 4B)), highlighting the value of EM-DINO pretraining in providing robust, transferable embeddings for mitochondria 222 

segmentation. Overall, OmniEM gives the best average dice 78.77%, compared to MitoNet (71.87%) and nnU-Net (63.34%) 223 

(Figure 4B). The largest improvement occurs on the salivary gland dataset, where mitochondria exhibit flat, bowl-shaped 224 

geometries, high organelle density, and weak contrast against salivary granules (Figure 4A, sixth column). In this particularly 225 

challenging case, OmniEM’s Dice score leaps from 11.27 % (MitoNet) to 42.59 % (Figure 4B, sixth inset), demonstrating 226 

that EM-DINO pretraining confers enhanced representational capacity for segmenting rare or low-contrast mitochondrial 227 

morphologies. On the 3D MitoEM benchmark, OmniEM achieved the highest performance (Dice = 93.63 %), outperforming 228 

nnU-Net (Dice = 90.23 %) and MedNeXt (Dice = 87.82 %) (Extended Figure 9)A & C. These results underscore OmniEM’s 229 

strong cross-dataset generalization performance, even on unseen tissue types and diverse imaging conditions. 230 

Compared to single-organelle segmentation, multi-organelle segmentation is considerably more challenging due to the 231 

diverse morphologies, sizes, and close spatial relationships among different organelles. Variability in imaging conditions, stain-232 

ing quality, and the inherent ultrastructural complexity further complicate accurate delineation, often resulting in overlapping 233 

boundaries and ambiguous separations between adjacent structures. To address these challenges, we implemented a multi-class 234 

segmentation head within OmniEM and evaluated its performance on the BetaSeg dataset 19 , which includes annotations for 235 

Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, insulin secretory granules, and nuclei (all other pixels are treated as background). We further 236 

evaluated OmniEM under a zero-shot generalization setting by splitting the BetaSeg volumes into disjoint training (high_c1, 237 

high_c2, high_c3) and testing (high_c4, low_c1, low_c2, low_c3) sets. To assess robustness across dimensional settings, 238 

we conducted both 2D and 3D experiments by training on cropped 2D slices or 3D subvolumes, respectively. For baseline 239 

comparison, we selected GobletNet 41 , a recently 2D-only model that uses wavelet transforms to extract high-frequency features 240 

to improve segmentation accuracy , MedNeXt for 3D segmentation 39 , and nnU-Net 40 for both 2D and 3D settings. In addition, 241 

for 2D experiments, we subsampled the training data into four fractions, 1 %, 30 %, 60 %, and 100 %, to test few-shot learning 242 

capability. 243

unseen volumes, achieving mean Dice scores above 70 %, whereas GobletNet remains substantially lower. OmniEM further 246 

attains the highest overall performance in this regime, particularly improving segmentation of Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, 247 

and nuclei. Across increasing data availability (30–100 %), OmniEM maintains stable and consistent gains over nnU-Net in 248

Integrating OmniEM into Scalable EM Workflows 260 

OmniEM’s multi-task design enables streamlined, end-to-end EM workflows by combining advanced restoration, super-261 

resolution, and segmentation within a single, user-friendly framework. We have packaged OmniEM’s restoration and 262 

segmentation modules into an interactive Napari plugin (Fig. 5), allowing users to apply OmniEM via a graphical interface. 263 

To accommodate the diverse characteristics of EM datasets, including small- or large-field images and both 2D and 3D 264 

modalities, we provide two inference modes in the OmniEM–Napari plugin. The first supports in-memory, single-GPU 265 

inference for small-scale datasets, whereas the second enables local, multi-GPU inference for large-scale slices and volumes by 266 

leveraging Dask-based lazy loading instead of fully loading data into memory. These complementary modes allow users to 267 

flexibly load data, adjust parameters according to available hardware resources, and interactively visualize results through the 268 

Napari graphical interface. Using the glycolytic muscle mitochondrial segmentation benchmark as an example, we contrast 269 

this integrated workflow with the conventional paradigm that focuses on isolated processing steps, where restoration and 270 

segmentation are performed using separate models. In contrast, the OmniEM plugin supports a more realistic end-to-end 271 

workflow by sequentially combining restoration and segmentation on the same dataset, resulting in a substantial improvement 272 

in segmentation performance in this dataset, with the Dice score increasing from 28 .94% to 74 .04% when restoration is applied 273 

prior to segmentation. Similar workflows can be constructed for other datasets and tasks using the same plugin interface. By 274 

bundling pretrained EM-DINO and OmniEM checkpoints within this plugin, we provide a comprehensive and reproducible 275 

workflow for scalable, high-quality EM analysis with minimal technical overhead. 276 

## Discussion 277 

In this work, we demonstrate that large-scale, EM-specific self-supervised learning enables the construction of a foundational 278 

vision model capable of unifying previously fragmented EM workflows. At the core of our approach is a novel data-curation 279 

pipeline that systematically deduplicates and filters vEM patches, reducing redundancy while keeping biological and technical 280 

diversity. By pairing EM-DINO for robust feature extraction with OmniEM for flexible downstream prediction tasks, we deliver 281 

a suite of tools tailored to the EM community’s demand for efficient, scalable, and reproducible analysis. Although achieving 282 

maximizing benchmark scores was not our primary objective, OmniEM nonetheless attains strong performance in denoising, 283 

super-resolution, and segmentation without reliance on handcrafted heuristics or specialized architectures, highlighting the 284 

generalization power of EM-DINO’s learned representations. 285 

Methodology 286 

A central but often underappreciated component of this work is the data curation strategy underlying EM-5M. Volume electron 287 

microscopy data are characterized by extreme redundancy, with neighboring slices often differing only marginally at the pixel 288 

level. To address this, we adopted an iterative data reduction recipe: an initial EM-DINO model was pretrained and used to 289 

identify and suppress redundant image content, after which newly added data were incorporated and the model retrained. As a 290 

result, EM-5M should not be interpreted as merely a collection of 5 million images, but rather as a distilled representation 291 

derived from more than 500 TB of raw EM data, retaining high structural diversity while mitigating redundancy. Data diversity 292 

was treated as a primary design constraint. Although available EM data are heavily skewed toward brain tissue (driven by 293 

connectomics) and mouse samples (notably from OpenOrganelle), we explicitly constrained any single domain to contribute 294 

no more than 50% of the dataset and excluded several large but highly homogeneous sources (e.g., MICrONS 20 ) to avoid 295 

overdominance. Importantly, we empirically evaluated pretraining on substantially larger but unreduced datasets and observed 296 

inferior downstream performance, indicating that scale alone is insufficient; rather, controlling redundancy and promoting 297 

structural diversity are critical factors for effective EM representation learning. Together, these observations suggest that data 298 

recipe (how EM data are selected, balanced, and distilled) are as important as model architecture or training objective for 299 

building generalizable EM foundation models. 300

Image representation learning, often realized through large-scale self-supervised pretraining, aims to extract meaningful 301 

and structured information to support multiple downstream analyses 42 . In natural image analysis, large-scale self-supervised 302 

learning frameworks such as MoCo 43 and SimCLR 44 have demonstrated that a single pretrained representation can generalize 303 

effectively across a wide range of downstream tasks, bridging traditional divides between recognition, segmentation, and 304 

reconstruction. These approaches highlight the value of learning transferable, task-agnostic visual embeddings from diverse 305 

data. In contrast, progress in the EM domain has largely followed task-specific trajectories. Most prior self-supervised methods, 306 

including CEM 13, 45 , SegNeuron 46 , and MASTER 47 , were designed to enhance performance for particular segmentation 307 

benchmarks, rather than to establish a unified representation capable of supporting multiple EM analysis tasks. In this work, we 308 

build upon DINOv2 as the pretraining strategy, as it combines masked image modeling with contrastive self-distillation and has 309 

demonstrated strong transferability across vision tasks. 310 

The EM-DINO/OmniEM framework employs a hybrid pretraining–training strategy: EM-DINO performs purely 2D self-311 

supervised pretraining to learn generalizable image representations, while OmniEM uses a hybrid architecture that combines 312 

2D encoders with task-specific 2D or 3D decoders, enabling both 2D and 3D downstream applications. In contrast, most 313 

existing self-supervised frameworks operate in a single data dimensionality: many are strictly 2D, as in natural images 43, 48 ,314 

whole-slide imaging 49 , and EM-specific methods 13, 47 , whereas others are fully 3D for biomedical image pretraining 46, 50 .315 

Our mixed design reflects the nature of EM data, which spans both slice-based 2D analyses and volumetric 3D tasks, yet 316 

often lacks well-curated and fully aligned 3D representations. Many EM workflows remain inherently 2D, including image 317 

alignment 51 and subcellular anatomy studies 52 , while volumetric data are common in fields requiring large-scale analysis, such 318 

as connectomics 6. Accordingly, EM-DINO/OmniEM operates directly on raw 2D EM images without needing complete 3D 319 

volumes, yet effectively transfers to 3D tasks through OmniEM. This design positions EM-DINO/OmniEM as a flexible and 320 

generalizable representation learning framework that complements existing 2D- or 3D-specific approaches and accommodates 321 

the heterogeneous data formats typical of EM studies. 322 

Downstream tasks 323 

EM restoration plays a pivotal role in EM-based research and is a critical step toward enabling high-throughput EM imaging. 324 

Recent advances in deep learning–based methods have substantially outperformed conventional image restoration approaches 53 ,325 

but challenges remain. A key issue is balancing high-quality restoration with image fidelity. Regression-based models, such 326 

as PSSR 35, 36 , are effective at suppressing noise but often lead to over-smoothing, resulting in the loss of fine ultrastructural 327 

details. Conversely, diffusion-based methods pioneered by EMDiffuse 15 address over-smoothing but tend to over-interpret the 328 

data, introducing visually plausible yet physiologically incorrect structures. In the absence of reference images, such artifacts 329 

are difficult to distinguish and may lead to erroneous biological conclusions. In this work, we address this by incorporating 330 

embeddings from pretrained EM-DINO into the training loss, achieving a practical balance between detail recovery and 331 

reconstruction fidelity across various noise levels. This strategy enables OmniEM to restore fine ultrastructural features while 332 

avoiding spurious structural artifacts, improving the reliability and biological plausibility of the reconstructed images. In 333 

parallel, our restoration results also highlight limitations in evaluation metrics for EM. Commonly used pixel-based metrics 334 

for natural images, such as PSNR and SSIM, have been shown to be inadequate for assessing EM image quality 15 . Although 335 

perceptual and structural metrics such as LPIPS and FSIM provide more informative assessments, they are not specifically 336 

designed for EM data. For example, LPIPS relies on feature representations learned from natural images, and the substantial 337 

domain gap between natural and EM images limits the appropriateness of directly applying such models. We believe EM-DINO 338 

can provide a promising starting point for EM-specific evaluation. In short, developing robust metrics and perceptual models 339 

tailored to EM remains an important direction for future work. 340 

EM segmentation has traditionally been dominated by U-shaped convolutional architectures and their variants, with recent 341 

work exploring attention- and state-space–based extensions that primarily target improved performance under matched training 342 

and testing conditions 14, 54, 55 . In contrast, this study places particular emphasis on robustness under distribution shift, which is 343 

a pervasive challenge in practical EM analysis. On the BetaSeg benchmark, where training and testing data originate from 344 

the same imaging series and species, OmniEM performs comparably to established pipelines such as nnU-Net. However, 345 

on cross-dataset and cross-species mitochondrial benchmarks spanning multiple datasets, imaging conditions, and species, 346 

OmniEM exhibits more stable performance. We attribute this behavior not to architectural novelty alone, but to the use 347 

of EM-specific self-supervised pretraining combined with a lightweight task-adaptation architecture, which encourages the 348 

model to learn transferable ultrastructural features rather than task- or dataset-specific cues. This distinction is especially 349 

important in EM, where expert annotations are expensive and limited, and models are frequently applied to unlabeled data 350 

that differ substantially from the available training distributions. By integrating self-supervised representation learning with 351 

supervised task adaptation, the EM-DINO/OmniEM framework offers a practical balance between in-distribution performance 352 

and cross-dataset generalization, addressing a central bottleneck in scalable EM image analysis. 353

Limitations and future extensions 354 

Because most modern image processing and interpretation pipelines hinge on the availability of suitable features, EM-DINO 355 

can serve as a universal feature extractor, readily integrated into existing tools to enhance performance. For example, in image 356 

stitching and alignment, point correspondence detection often depends on classical descriptors such as SIFT 56 , or more recently, 357 

deep encoders trained to suppress artifacts in EM images 16, 20 . EM-DINO’s semantically enriched features could significantly 358 

improve matching accuracy across sections, particularly under varying imaging conditions. Similarly, in connectomics pipelines 359 

that require the linking of neurite fragments, cellular identity assignment, and synapse detection, practitioners typically rely on 360 

separate classifiers optimized for each task 57 . We propose that EM-DINO can serve as a unified representation backbone across 361 

these components, reducing task fragmentation and simplifying pipeline integration. 362 

While EM-DINO provides versatile and semantically rich features for diverse tasks, representation learning alone does 363 

not equate to full biological understanding of EM data. EM images span large fields of view and volumetric contexts, and 364 

image-only self-supervised pretraining lacks explicit biological semantics, such as organelle or cellular identities 2. We therefore 365 

view representation learning as a foundational step toward large-field EM image understanding and semantic alignment across 366 

datasets and modalities. By providing open models and tools for learning EM representations, this work establishes a basis for 367 

future integration with higher-level biological inference. For example, strategies for aggregating local 2D image embeddings 368 

could be used to construct global representations of large-field images, similar to approaches previously applied in whole-slide 369 

imaging 58 . Beyond EM alone, EM-DINO could serve as a modular component for multimodal integration, linking EM data 370 

with complementary modalities such as cryo-electron tomography, light microscopy 59 or even natural language annotations. 371 

Taken together, these directions highlight the potential of EM-DINO as a foundational image-only feature extractor for EM 372 

data, paving the way toward more comprehensive and integrative analyses of cellular ultrastructure. 373 

Looking ahead, several strategies could further strengthen EM-DINO’s utility, improving both its generalization across 374 

diverse EM datasets and its computational efficiency. First, expanding EM-5M beyond its current emphasis on brain and 375 

mouse vEM to include underrepresented organs (e.g., kidney, heart, bone) and non-mammalian species will ensure downstream 376 

models learn truly transferable representations across diverse EM applications. To facilitate this, we have made our data 377 

curation tools publicly available, enabling the community to contribute additional datasets and further enrich EM-5M. Second, 378 

although EM-DINO uses a Vision Transformer backbone, alternative architectures, such as hierarchical transformers like Swin 379 

Transformer 60 or Mamba 61 , may better capture EM’s anisotropic resolution and high noise levels. Systematic benchmarking 380 

of these backbones on EM-specific tasks will clarify which design choices maximize ultrastructural detail. Finally, EM-381 

DINO’s large-scale pretraining yields robust embeddings, but fine-tuning remains computationally intense; parameter-efficient 382 

techniques like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) 62 offer a promising route to rapid adaptation on new datasets with minimal 383 

overhead. Additionally, OmniEM currently employs separate branches for restoration and segmentation, missing potential 384 

synergies from shared features. A multi-task or auxiliary-learning framework could unify these tasks, improving both efficiency 385 

and accuracy 63 , and moving closer to a truly general-purpose, dense-prediction model for EM data. By addressing these 386 

challenges, future work can build on our EM-specific foundation to create ever more versatile, accurate, and efficient tools for 387 

nanoscale biological discovery. 388
